[Ren et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.13104#page=12) say it best: an agent is made up of two parts, the model and its scaffold.[^agents]

:::: {.agent-equation}
::: {.agent-formula}
$$
\mathcal{A}_t = (
  \class{parametric-term}{\theta_t},
  \class{nonparametric-term}{\Sigma_t}
)
$$
:::

::: {.equation-labels}
::: {.equation-label .parametric-label}
**Parametric**

Model weights
:::

::: {.equation-label .nonparametric-label}
**Non-parametric**

Prompts, memory, tools, [control code]{.component-name}

[Fan et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.20804#page=3){.component-citation}
:::
:::

::::

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) methods typically focus on improving one of these two parts. The parametric vs. non-parametric breakdown tells us **what part of the system is improved**. We can also ask **why the agent is making changes**:

- **Discovery:** To explore and discover new concepts and skills.
  - example: learning what questions to ask to uncover gaps in its knowledge
- **Direct optimization:** To improve the task metric directly.[^goodhart]
  - example: adjusting prompts to improve a benchmark score

Both approaches are still measured by their performance on the task. 

Utility learning agents learn a utility $V(s;\theta_t,\Sigma_t)$ over situations.[^utility] The weights and scaffold determine how the current agent evaluates a situation. Its ideal planning rule is:

$$
\mathcal{A}_t(s_0) = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\mathbf{u}}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[V(s_T;\theta_t,\Sigma_t) - K(\mathbf{u})\right]
$$

Here, $\mathcal{A}_t(s_0)$ denotes the action sequence $\mathbf{u}$ selected by the agent from situation $s_0$. The search is over plans it can execute; $s_T$ is the resulting situation, including its history, and $K$ is the plan's cost. Learning this utility can change the model weights $\theta_t$, the scaffold $\Sigma_t$, or both.
